Split the days until 8 November into three blocks: review of what you have already studied, practice tests with error correction, and two light final days. Each day gets a list of at most 4 concrete tasks, for example: 1 essay, 20 maths questions, 1 humanities topic and correcting yesterday's mistakes. Keep today's list in a Home Screen widget and tick each item as soon as it is done.

The final stretch before ENEM has a familiar problem: the schedule made in March fell behind, and the urge is to make up for everything at once. A 15-task day looks responsible in the morning and turns into guilt at night. With 4 tasks you can see the end of the day, each tick is a quarter of the way, and the next morning you actually want to open the list again. Over the next weeks, consistency beats one heroic Sunday.

A split that works for 17 days: days 1 to 7, review the topics you have already studied and that show up most in past papers; this is not the time to start a new subject from scratch. Days 8 to 15, a practice test or a past paper every other day, always with the following day kept for correcting mistakes. Days 16 and 17 stay light: summaries, enough sleep, official photo ID and a black ballpoint pen with a transparent barrel set aside, and the route to the test site checked.

ENEM runs on two Sundays, 8 and 15 November, and that changes the schedule. The first covers Languages, Human Sciences and the essay; the second, Natural Sciences and Mathematics. So the essay and humanities come first now, and the week between the two days belongs to the sciences: one short list per day with maths, physics, chemistry and biology questions, without trying to review everything.

A task only goes on the list if it fits in under an hour and you can tell when it is done. "Study biology" does not work; "redo the 10 ecology questions I got wrong" does. "Practise the essay" becomes "write 1 essay on a past-paper topic and check it against the 5 competencies". If a task is left over at night, it does not roll over by itself: decide whether it goes on tomorrow's list or leaves for good.

A list only works if you see it. On iPhone that means a Home Screen widget rather than an app buried in a folder; with an Apple Watch you can follow your tasks on your wrist without picking up the phone and drifting into social media mid-study. Keep reminders for things with a time: Saturday's practice test, the prep-course class. Mochi is built around that gesture: write the task, tap the little circle when it is done. The steps that actually work

  1. Dump everything still worrying you into a "Review later" list: subjects, essay topics, past papers.
  2. Mark the dates: 8 November (Languages, Humanities and essay) and 15 November (Sciences and Maths).
  3. Create a "Today" list with at most 4 tasks, always including 1 essay or error-correction task.
  4. Break every vague task down until it fits in under an hour.
  5. Add the widget to your Home Screen and set reminders only for timed practice tests and classes.
  6. In the evening, tick what you did, delete what no longer makes sense, and pull 1 or 2 items up from "Review later".

FAQ

Isn't 4 tasks a day too few for ENEM?

In the final stretch, rarely. If you finish all 4, pull one more from "Review later". That is more motivating than starting with 12 and pushing 8 to tomorrow.

Is Mochi's free version enough?

For this method, yes: 2 lists with up to 4 tasks each, 5 themes and 1 emoji pack. Premium, an in-app purchase, unlocks unlimited lists and tasks and all 101 themes.

Can I see my tasks on an Apple Watch?

Yes. Mochi lets you follow your tasks on the Apple Watch, as well as through Home Screen widgets.

What about the week between the two exam days?

Make one short list per day with only Natural Sciences and Mathematics: past-paper questions and correcting mistakes. Rest goes on the list too.
