Write a one-page summary as conclusion, three points, next step: make the title a one-sentence conclusion, add three supporting points each with a number or fact where possible, and close with the one decision or action you need from the reader. Anything that does not fit that frame goes in an appendix, not onto the page.

The conclusion goes first because a one-page reader often has only a few seconds. "Q3 revenue report" is a topic, not a conclusion; "Q3 revenue grew 12 percent, mostly from new customers, but repeat purchases fell and need attention" is a conclusion. Make the title a full sentence so a reader who only sees the title still gets the point, and let the rest of the page prove it.

Cap the supporting points at three. Beyond that readers stop remembering and you start adding by-the-way details. Each point should answer "so what": put the number inside the sentence instead of pasting a table, use a line for trends, a side-by-side for comparisons and a flow for steps. Always end with a next step, such as "please decide by Friday whether to raise the budget", or the page becomes a report people forget.

What to check before you choose

  • Whether the title is a full-sentence conclusion, not just a topic
  • Whether supporting points are kept to three or fewer
  • Whether each point carries a number or fact instead of adjectives
  • Whether each chart type matches the data: trend, comparison, steps, dates
  • Whether the final line states the decision or next step you need

The steps that actually work

  1. Write the conclusion as one sentence and use it as the title
  2. Pick the three points from your material that best prove it
  3. Give each point a number, date or concrete fact
  4. Choose the visual by data type: line for trends, side-by-side for comparisons, flow for steps
  5. End with the decision or action you need, and move the rest to an appendix

FAQ

How is a one-page summary different from a digest?

A digest makes information easy to read; a one-page summary takes a position: conclusion first, points as proof, a decision at the end. Ask yourself what you want the reader to do after reading.

What if the material will not fit on one page?

Do not shrink fonts or charts. Fix the conclusion, keep only the three points that directly support it, and move the remaining detail to an appendix or notes.

Does a one-page summary need a chart?

No. Use a chart only when numbers really show a trend or difference; when the data cannot carry one, three clear sentences read better than a forced graphic.
