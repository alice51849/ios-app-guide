An iPhone works as a noise meter for orientation between roughly 30 and 100 dB if you use A weighting, read the average rather than the peak and calibrate against a reference meter; for evidence or occupational safety you need a certified instrument.

A noise meter reports sound pressure in decibels, and the scale is logarithmic: an extra 10 dB is perceived as roughly twice as loud, and an extra 3 dB already means double the sound energy. For reference, a quiet bedroom sits around 30 dB, a normal home in the daytime at 40 to 50, conversation at about 60, heavy traffic at 70 to 80, and a concert near the stage above 100. What matters is not the single spike but the average over the time you are exposed.

The second question is weighting. A weighting mimics human hearing and is what occupational limits and residential guidelines use; C weighting keeps low frequencies and says more about bass or machinery hum; Z is unweighted. Only compare your reading with limits that use the same weighting. In most occupational rules the action levels sit around 80 and 85 dB(A) as a daily average, while neighbourhood noise is judged against guideline values that depend on the area type and the time of day.

A phone microphone is built for speech, not measurement. It compresses at very high levels, shows mostly its own noise floor in quiet rooms and is not calibrated out of the box. Treat the values as estimates and calibrate against a reference meter if you need more than orientation. dB Halo shows the live level in its Halo ring with minimum, average, maximum and peak, lets you choose A, C or Z weighting, accepts an external microphone and saves multiple calibration profiles, and states itself that its measurements are estimates and that it is not a certified sound level meter.

What to check before you choose

  • Which weighting (A, C or Z) the app shows, and whether you compare with the same one
  • Whether average, maximum and peak are shown separately, not just a momentary value
  • Whether the microphone can be calibrated against a reference meter
  • Whether a time-stamped event log is kept to document when noise happened
  • Whether processing stays on the device, with no account, ads or tracking

The steps that actually work

  1. Choose A weighting when comparing with guideline or action values, C for bass or hum.
  2. Hold the iPhone without a case at ear height, microphone towards the source, not covered by your hand.
  3. Measure for at least several minutes and read the average, not the peak.
  4. Note the time and situation, or let a time-stamped log run alongside.
  5. For evidence or occupational safety, repeat with a certified meter or have it measured professionally.

FAQ

How accurate is an iPhone as a noise meter?

Usable for orientation in the middle range, better after calibrating against a reference meter. At very quiet and very loud levels a phone microphone deviates more, and for authorities or occupational safety only a certified instrument counts.

At what dB does noise become dangerous?

Occupational rules put the upper action level around 85 dB(A) as a daily average; the louder it is, the shorter the safer duration. Short peaks matter less than total exposure over the day.

Does dB Halo save my recordings?

Not unless you ask it to. The Sleep Journal logs events without saving raw microphone audio, and an AAC file is created only when you explicitly start a recording. There is no account, no ads, no analytics and no tracking.
