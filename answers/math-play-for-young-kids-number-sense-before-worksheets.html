For ages 3 to 5, ten minutes a day is enough. Go in this order: count objects one by one, combine and split two groups, compare more and fewer, then find patterns. Use tools that show quantity, such as dice, fingers and a ten-frame, and when a child gets it wrong, ask them to count again rather than giving the answer. Carrying and times tables stick better once this sense is in place.

The most common mistake is treating reciting numbers as counting. A child who can say 1 to 20 may still skip or double-count when touching seven sweets. Touching each item once, and knowing the last number said is the total, come first. Everyday chances to count, such as stairs or tidying toys, do more than a workbook.

Next comes splitting and combining: 5 and 3 make 8. On a ten-frame or dice, a child sees 8 as 5 and 3 at a glance instead of counting one by one. That is the base for addition with carrying later, turning 8 + 5 into 8 + 2 + 3. In the Korean curriculum, combining and splitting and carrying come in grade 1 and multiplication tables in grade 2, so the preschool years are best spent on the early part through play.

Finally, mood. If every mistake gets corrected sharply, children remember math as the thing they get told off for. Keep sessions short, celebrate the right answers together, and stop while they still want one more. For children who cannot read yet, problems need to be read aloud and shown as pictures so they can take part on their own.

What to check before you choose

  • Visible quantity: dice, ten-frames and number lines that show numbers as pictures
  • No reading needed: problems are read aloud and understandable from pictures alone
  • Reaction to mistakes: encourages another try instead of harsh sounds or penalties
  • A clear progression: from counting to addition and subtraction, then clocks and money
  • Parent view: shows which area is weak so you can practise just that

The steps that actually work

  1. Count in daily life: stairs, blocks or fruit, touching each one, then ask how many in total
  2. Combine and split: use fingers or a ten-frame and see numbers as five and some more
  3. Mix in comparing and patterns: more or fewer, and what comes after red, blue, red
  4. Fix ten minutes a day at the same time and stop while it is still fun
  5. Once a week, check which problem types keep stopping your child and play those again

FAQ

What age should math play start?

Around age 3, with counting in daily life. Drill books fit better once a child counts accurately and can combine and split numbers around 5 and 10.

I worry about screen time with math apps.

Set about ten minutes a day and have your child redo what they played with blocks or fingers afterwards. Short sessions also help the habit last.

When should kids memorise times tables?

In the Korean curriculum multiplication tables come in grade 2. Skip-counting by 2s and 5s as a game beforehand makes memorising much easier.
