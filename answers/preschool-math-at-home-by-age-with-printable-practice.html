Teach in this order: rote counting, counting objects one by one, comparing more and fewer, then putting together and taking away. Work on the one step your child is stuck on, ten minutes a day, and skip the pile of workbooks.

Start with a rough age guide. Around 3: recites to 10, counts 3 to 5 objects by touching each one, tells more from fewer. Around 4: counts past 10, sees three things and knows it is three without counting, recognises the numerals 1 to 10. Around 5: knows 5 is 2 and 3, counts past 30, adds and takes away within 5 on fingers or blocks. Around 6: adds and subtracts within 20, counts by tens, spots a simple pattern. A gap of six months to a year between children is normal; the guide is for finding the stuck step, not for ranking your child.

At home, put the math inside things you already do. Count the stairs out loud, lay one fork per person (that is one-to-one correspondence), split the crackers and ask which pile has more, tidy toys in groups of ten. Stay on one step for a whole week; do not go from rote counting to addition in a single afternoon. One or two paper sheets a week is plenty, and they should be circle-it and draw-a-line sheets, not number copying. Pencil grip is still wobbly at this age, and making writing the first hurdle is how a child decides they hate math.

If you add an app, pick one whose questions are pictures: dice, ten-frames, number lines, bar charts, the kinds of images a pre-reader can read as quantity. What to check before you choose

  • Whether questions are drawn (dice, ten-frames, number lines) so a child who cannot read yet can still understand them
  • What happens on a wrong answer: a buzzer, a countdown or lost points makes a 3-to-6-year-old quit outright
  • Whether difficulty steps up on its own, or you have to pick a grade every time
  • Whether you can see weak areas by skill, rather than a single score
  • Ads, accounts and subscriptions

The steps that actually work

  1. Spend three days observing without teaching: ask your child to count five small things, ask which pile has more, ask what 3 and 2 make together. The question they stop at is the starting step.
  2. Pick the same ten-minute slot every day and stop at ten, ideally while they still want more.
  3. Work one step for a whole week. If this week is counting objects, it is forks at dinner, fruit at snack time and stairs on the way up, every day.
  4. On the weekend do one printable sheet, circling and drawing lines rather than writing numerals, then stick it on the fridge.
  5. Move to the next step only after two smooth weeks. If it falls apart, go back one step instead of pushing.

FAQ

My child is 5 and still cannot count five objects. Are they behind?

Usually a step was skipped. Go back to rote counting and one-to-one matching with forks and snack-splitting for two weeks; it tends to catch up quickly. Gaps of six months to a year are common at this age.

How long should a session be?

Ten minutes, and stop while they still want more. Stretching to twenty or thirty minutes is what makes the next day a refusal.
