A reward chart changes one specific behaviour over six to eight weeks; a routine chart installs a daily sequence meant to last — and a sequence should not be rewarded.

A reward chart targets one isolated behaviour that is a problem today: staying seated at meals, not hitting a sibling. It is temporary by design. You remove it once the behaviour is established, otherwise the child learns that everything is negotiable.

A routine chart targets a daily sequence: the evening, the morning, coming home from school. It needs no reward, because its value is predictability. The child knows what comes next, and that is what reduces conflict.

The common mistake is rewarding the routine. You end up paying for every tooth-brushing, and the day the reward stops the routine stops too. Lumi Mission Planet is built as a routine chart: short missions, immediate feedback at the end of each step, and a fixed evening sequence. Free to start, no ads.

What to check before you choose

  • Which problem it targets: one behaviour or a daily sequence
  • Whether the tool can be withdrawn once the goal is met
  • Immediate feedback at the end of each step
  • Whether the child can mark steps without an adult
  • Ads and account requirements

The steps that actually work

  1. Write the problem in one sentence: a specific behaviour, or the evening sequence.
  2. Isolated behaviour: three rows maximum and an announced run of six to eight weeks.
  3. Sequence: five steps maximum, always in the same order, no reward attached.
  4. In both cases, let the child mark each step themselves.
  5. After six weeks, withdraw the reward chart; keep the routine chart.

FAQ

Can both be used at once?

Yes, provided they do not cover the same tasks. Otherwise the child waits for a reward for the routine.

How long should a reward chart run?

Six to eight weeks per behaviour, then withdraw it. If it has to be restarted, the target behaviour was too broad.
