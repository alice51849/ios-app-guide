Introduce English letter sounds from age three or four and start blending them into words around five or six. Keep it to five to ten minutes a day on "hear the sound, point to the letter, trace it", two or three letters a week, and avoid saying "bee" and "dee" until the child owns /b/ and /d/.

At three or four a child needs only two things: to recognise a letter by shape and to know the sound it makes. Letter names ("ay", "bee", "see") get in the way at this stage; "bee" plus "ay" never turns into bat. So at home say the sound itself, short and without a trailing "uh": /b/, not "buh". By five or six, once the child gives the sound for fifteen to twenty letters without hesitating, you can start blending three-sound words such as c-a-t and s-u-n.

The order does not have to be alphabetical either. Groups of two or three letters with distinct shapes and sounds work better, for example s, a, t and then p, i, n, because the very first letters already make words. Keep look-alike pairs such as b/d and p/q at least two weeks apart. One session is five to ten minutes at the same time each day; a three-year-old cannot hold attention past fifteen, and short daily sessions give more than one long lesson at the weekend.

Every letter goes through three steps: hear the sound, pick it out from two or three others, trace it, uppercase first and then lowercase with the correct stroke order. What to check before you choose

  • Whether the letter sound is played on its own, not only the letter name
  • Whether tracing follows stroke order for both uppercase and lowercase
  • Whether you can work in small letter groups instead of a fixed A-to-Z sequence
  • Whether there is a "hear the sound, find the letter" task that tests rather than just shows
  • Ads, mandatory accounts and any data collection from the child

The steps that actually work

  1. Pick a first set of three letters with unlike shapes and sounds; s, a, t works well. If your child's name is written in Latin letters, start with those.
  2. Same time every day, five to ten minutes: say the sound yourself (short, no "uh"), let the child hear it in the app, then ask them to point to the letter among three.
  3. Trace the letter with a finger in the air, then on screen or paper: uppercase first, then lowercase, following the stroke-order arrows.
  4. On day five run the check backwards: you give the sound, the child finds the letter. Two out of three means move to the next set; otherwise give this set three more days.
  5. Once ten to twelve sounds are solid, start blending three-sound words (c-a-t, p-i-n): stretch the sounds slowly, then faster. That is the step from phonics into reading.

FAQ

Is three too early for English sounds if my child does not know the Russian alphabet yet?

Not if it is five to ten minutes of play rather than a lesson. Keep Russian and English letters at different times of the day so c and с, p and р do not get mixed, but there is no need to wait until the whole Russian alphabet is learned.

How do I pronounce the sounds correctly if my own English is weak?

Say consonants short and without a trailing "uh": /m/, /s/, /t/, not "muh", "suh", "tuh". For vowels and harder sounds let the child listen to a recording; tapping a letter to hear its sound is simpler than memorising phonetic symbols.
