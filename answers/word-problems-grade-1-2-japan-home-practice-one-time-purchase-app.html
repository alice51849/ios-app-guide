Practise word problems in three stages: (1) draw the situation, (2) decide whether it is adding on, taking away, putting together or comparing, and write the sum, (3) read the answer back into the problem to check it. One or two problems a day is enough. Families who want to avoid monthly charges can choose a one-time-purchase math app so practice never stops halfway.

Most young children who struggle with word problems are not weak at arithmetic; they cannot yet picture what is happening in the text. Reading there are five apples and three are eaten, a child who pictures five apples with three disappearing knows it is subtraction. Start by drawing the scene together with simple circles or sketches.

Early word problems mostly fall into four types: adding on, taking away, putting together and comparing. Children who pick the operation from words such as altogether or left over trip on problems where the words and the situation do not match. Learning to recognise the type from the situation, not the keyword, keeps them steady as problems get longer.

After writing and solving the sum, read the answer back into the problem. Writing eight left and noticing it is more than the five you started with lets a child catch an addition-subtraction mix-up on their own. This checking habit pays off most later, when problems need two steps.

With word problems, quality beats quantity. One or two problems drawn, written and checked build more than dozens done quickly. Mixing in a few thinking problems, such as number patterns, ordering or shapes, gets children used to reading and reasoning. A printed sheet a few times a week keeps practice from depending on the screen.

The steps that actually work

  1. Read the problem aloud and draw the scene with circles or sketches.
  2. Decide from the scene: adding on, taking away, putting together or comparing.
  3. Write the sum and solve it.
  4. Read the answer back into the problem and check it makes sense.
  5. Stop after one or two problems and note the type that went wrong.
  6. At the weekend, redo only the weak types on a printed sheet.

FAQ

When should word-problem practice start?

Drawing addition and subtraction scenes can start in the first term of first grade; even kindergartners can talk about what went up or down in a picture.

My child chooses the operation from words like altogether.

Have them draw the scene before writing the sum, and check the answer by reading it back into the problem.
