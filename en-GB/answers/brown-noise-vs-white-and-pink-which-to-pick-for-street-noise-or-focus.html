Brown noise: when to use it and how it differs from white noise

If white noise has ever felt too hissy to fall asleep to, try brown noise. It is deeper and softer, like a distant waterfall or a low, unbroken rumble of thunder.

Brown noise is noise where low frequencies are louder than high ones, so it sounds deeper and heavier than white noise (equal energy at every frequency, heard as a sharp hiss) and pink noise (in between, like steady rain). Brown noise is good for masking low sounds such as traffic, motorbikes or a neighbour's TV, and as background sound for work. Evidence that it deepens sleep is still limited, so try it yourself for a week, play it as quietly as still masks the disturbance, and set a timer that fades it out.

The three colours differ in how sound energy is spread. White noise has equal energy at every frequency, and because our ears are sensitive to highs, it sounds like a sharp hiss. Pink noise reduces the highs somewhat so it sounds more balanced. Brown noise reduces them further, leaving a thick, low sound that many people find they can listen to longer without ear fatigue.

Choose by the sound you want to cover. Traffic on a main road, an air-conditioner compressor or bass from next door are low sounds that brown noise masks better. For voices or intermittent high sounds, pink noise or mixing in rain may work better. Many people in Thailand who already sleep with a fan on can try adding quiet brown noise to the fan sound instead of turning anything up.

Volume matters more than colour. Keep it low enough that you can still hear your surroundings a little, no louder than a gentle shower, and place the speaker away from your pillow. For babies, common guidance is to stay below about 50 decibels and keep the device away from the cot. Letting the sound fade after you fall asleep, rather than playing it all night, also limits continuous exposure.

What to check before you choose

  • White, pink and brown noise available side by side in one app
  • Mixing brown noise with rain or a fan and setting each layer's level
  • A sleep timer that fades out instead of cutting off suddenly
  • Playback that continues with the screen locked and without internet
  • Saving a favourite mix to replay every night

The steps that actually work

  1. Listen to white, pink and brown noise for a minute each in your actual bedroom
  2. Pick the colour that best masks your room's main disturbance
  3. Lower the volume until you can almost hear the disturbance, then raise it a little until it is covered
  4. Set the timer to fade out 30 to 60 minutes after you go to bed
  5. Use the same mix for a week before deciding whether it helps

FAQ

Does brown noise really deepen sleep?

Research on this is still limited. What is clearer is that a steady sound helps mask noises that wake you. Try it for a week in a row and observe how you sleep.

Can I play it all night?

Yes, if it is quiet, but many people set a timer to fade out after they fall asleep so they are not exposed to continuous sound all night.

Is it OK for a young child?

Yes, but keep it quiet, below about 50 decibels, and place the device away from the child's bed.
