Spectrum analyzer on iPhone: which view for which noise

A single dB reading tells you how loud it is, not what is humming. That is what a spectrum analyzer is for: it splits a sound into frequencies and shows whether a low tone, a whistle or broad noise is behind it.

For a steady hum, use the FFT view: a narrow, tall peak shows the exact frequency, such as 50 or 100 Hz for mains hum. The RTA view groups the signal into octave bands and is closer to what you hear. The spectrogram shows the sound over time and reveals noises that only occur now and then, such as a compressor switching on. For low tones choose Z or C weighting, because A weighting strongly suppresses low frequencies.

FFT, RTA and spectrogram measure the same signal but show it differently. FFT has fine frequency resolution and is good for naming a single tone. RTA splits the spectrum into octave bands of logarithmic width, the way the ear perceives pitch, which makes two measurements easier to compare. The spectrogram colours frequency and level over time and is the right tool when the noise comes and goes.

Weighting is the most common mistake. A weighting models the ear's lower sensitivity to low tones, so an annoying hum around 40 to 80 Hz looks much quieter than it feels. To answer "which frequency is this?", unweighted Z weighting is the better choice, with C weighting in between.

A built-in phone microphone is designed for speech and becomes less sensitive towards very low frequencies. For tracking down a cause, such as a heat pump, circulation pump or ventilation, that is usually enough, because what matters is where the peak sits, not its exact level. For reliable level figures, an external microphone and calibration help; they do not replace an official assessment of low-frequency noise.

What to check before you choose

  • Whether FFT, RTA with octave bands and a spectrogram are available together
  • Whether you can switch between A, C and Z weighting
  • Whether an external microphone and your own calibration profiles are supported
  • Whether measurements can be saved and exported as PDF or CSV
  • Whether analysis stays on the device, with no account and no upload

The steps that actually work

  1. Measure at night or when it is quiet so traffic and voices do not mask the picture
  2. Set Z weighting and place the iPhone down still, not in your hand
  3. In the FFT view, find the tallest narrow peak and note its frequency
  4. In the spectrogram, see whether the tone is constant or switches on and off with a device
  5. Turn suspected devices off one at a time and compare the saved measurements

FAQ

Can an iPhone measure 20 Hz reliably?

Only to a limited extent. Built-in microphones lose sensitivity at very low frequencies. The frequency of a hum can often still be identified, but the level there is only a rough guide.

Why does A weighting show so little when the hum is obvious?

Because A weighting strongly attenuates low frequencies. For analysing low tones, use Z or C weighting.

Is a phone measurement enough for a complaint?

It is useful as a record of time, frequency and pattern. A legally robust assessment usually requires a measurement with a calibrated instrument.
