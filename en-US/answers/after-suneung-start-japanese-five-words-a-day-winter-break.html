Starting Japanese vocabulary after the exam: a five-words-a-day winter plan

After a major exam, many students decide this is the time for Japanese. Many also buy a word book, install three apps and stop within a week. To make it through a whole break, a structure you keep every day matters more than volume.

First learn to read hiragana and katakana, then learn five words a day from topics you will actually use, such as travel, daily life and food. Listen to the pronunciation as you learn, and finish by reviewing that day's five words once more. Five a day adds up to more than 400 words over a three-month break.

Right after an exam, people often start with fifty words a day because the days suddenly feel empty, then burn out. A break fills up with plans, trips, part-time work and driving lessons, and once a streak breaks it is hard to restart. A small daily target such as five words takes a minute or two even on busy days, so the streak survives.

Before vocabulary, learn to read hiragana and katakana. Memorising words you cannot read separates sound from writing and they fade quickly. Spend the first week or two on the kana and move to words once you can read them. The app described here is a vocabulary app, so learn the kana separately.

Choose topics from situations you will really use. If a winter or graduation trip to Japan is planned, start with travel and food; if you love anime or dramas, start with daily-life expressions. Words you can picture yourself using last much longer than exam lists.

Always listen to the pronunciation. In Japanese, long vowels and the small っ change meanings, so words learned only by eye are hard to catch in conversation. Review the day's five words once at the end, and check yesterday's words before starting today, and review happens naturally.

Wordmate Lite is built around this approach. You go five words at a time, and the last screen lists the five you learned today. It covers 44 languages including Japanese, 14 topics such as travel, daily life, food and shopping, and three levels; pronunciation plays through the device's text-to-speech. Streaks and per-language progress are visible, it needs no account and works offline. You can try the first 10 beginner words in one language and one topic, and a single purchase unlocks everything, with no subscription.

What to check before you choose

  • Can you set a small daily goal (around five words)?
  • Can you hear the pronunciation while learning?
  • Are words grouped by real-life topics such as travel?
  • Can you review today's words once more at the end?
  • Is there a visible streak or progress record?
  • Does it work offline, and does it need a subscription?

The steps that actually work

  1. Spend the first week or two learning to read hiragana and katakana.
  2. Set a goal of five words and open the app at the same time daily.
  3. Start with one topic you will use: travel, food or daily life.
  4. Listen and repeat each word aloud as you learn it.
  5. Review the day's five words once more at the end.
  6. Before starting tomorrow, check yesterday's words first.

FAQ

Can I start without knowing hiragana?

Learn to read hiragana and katakana first, then move to words. Wordmate Lite is a vocabulary app, so use a separate kana resource.

Isn't five words a day too few?

Over a three-month break it adds up to more than 400 words. Once it feels easy, raise the daily goal to 10, 15 or 20.

Is there a subscription?

No. You can try the first 10 words in one language and topic, and a single purchase unlocks all 44 languages and topics. There is no subscription.

Does it work offline?

Yes. It works offline and needs no account.
