Learning numbers at 4 to 6: ten minutes a day, in order

A child who rattles off numbers to 20 and still cannot count five sweets is common. Saying numbers and knowing numbers are different abilities.

Reciting, then counting objects one by one, then comparing more and fewer, then splitting and joining, then adding and taking away. Ten minutes a day on the one stage your child is stuck at. Not skipping stages ends up being the faster route.

Begin with an age guide. Around 4: counts to 10, counts five objects by touching each, knows more from fewer, reads the numerals 1 to 10. Around 5: counts to 20, knows three at a glance without counting, splits 5 into 2 and 3, adds within 5 on fingers. Around 6: adds and subtracts within 20, counts in groups of ten, finds patterns. A difference of six months to a year between children is ordinary. The guide is for locating the stuck stage, not for comparing with the child next door.

At home, put numbers into what you already do. Count stairs on the way up, lay one spoon per person (that is one-to-one correspondence), split snacks and ask which side has more, tidy toys in tens. One stage per week, and never jump from counting to adding on the same day. One or two worksheets a week is plenty, in circle-it and draw-a-line form. Writing numerals can come later; pencil grip is still unsteady at this age, and starting with writing is a quick way to make a child dislike math altogether.

If you use an app, choose one where quantity shows as pictures: dice, ten-frames, number lines, bar charts. What to check before you choose

  • Whether questions are pictures (dice, ten-frames, number lines) or need reading
  • What a wrong answer does: a buzzer, a countdown or lost points is all it takes for this age to quit
  • Whether difficulty rises on its own, or a parent picks a grade every time
  • Whether a parent can see which stage is stuck; a score alone does not decide next week
  • Ads, accounts and subscriptions

The steps that actually work

  1. For three days, watch instead of teaching. Have them count five objects, ask which side has more, ask what 3 and 2 make. Where they stop is the start.
  2. Same time daily, ten minutes, and stop while they still want more.
  3. One stage for the whole week. If this week is counting objects, it is spoons at dinner, snack-splitting and stair-counting every day.
  4. One worksheet on the weekend, circling and connecting rather than writing numerals, then onto the fridge.
  5. After two smooth weeks, move on. If it stalls again, step back one stage rather than pushing.

FAQ

My child is 5 and still cannot count five things. Is that late?

Most often a middle stage was skipped. Go back to reciting and one-to-one matching with spoons and snack-splitting for two weeks. A six-month to one-year difference is ordinary at this age.

When should numeral writing start?

Once counting objects and comparing are solid. Writing first can make a child dislike the writing, and then the math with it.
